The practiceWhere the difficult hours are

Neurological emergencies do not wait for office hours. Dr. Fillmore's work sits at the point where a stroke or a seizure disorder becomes a critical illness, and where the decisions made early shape everything after.

Neurocritical care

Care of patients whose neurological condition has become critical, from the emergency department through the intensive care unit.

Stroke medical direction

As Interim Stroke Medical Director he leads work on stroke protocols and patient care pathways at Presbyterian Medical Center.

Critical care EEG

Certified in critical care EEG, reading the electrical activity of the brain in patients who cannot tell anyone what they are experiencing.

Epilepsy monitoring

The work

Stroke, seizure and the first hours of critical illness

Three disciplines meet in one role: neurology, critical care and epilepsy. The hours that matter most are the ones nobody schedules.

At the bedside

The conversation is part of the treatment

In neurocritical care, families are asked to understand something frightening and decide quickly. Speaking four languages is not a line on a profile, it is the difference between a family being informed and a family being talked at. Dr. Fillmore's multilingual practice is aimed at that: communication with patients and families, so they receive care they can follow and understand during a critical illness.

TrainingA long route to the intensive care unit

Neurocritical care sits at the meeting point of three disciplines, and the training reflects that: a neurology residency first, then separate fellowships in critical care and in epilepsy. It is among the longer paths in medicine.

He is board certified in neurology by the American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology, and holds further certification from the American Board of Clinical Neurophysiology.
